Window management usually fades into the background. That is the goal, anyway: open what you need, put it somewhere useful, and get back to the work. Omarchy makes that small interaction feel unusually deliberate.

The interesting part is not simply that windows tile. It is that the desktop can express two different ideas of focus without asking you to rebuild your workspace.

<OmarchyWindowDemo />

## Dwindle makes the whole screen useful

Start with one window and it owns the workspace. Open a second and the screen splits into two equal columns. Open a third and the right-hand column splits again, placing the newest window underneath the second.

The result is predictable without feeling rigid. The first window keeps a large, stable area while newer tasks find space beside it. It works especially well when one thing is the anchor—an editor, a browser, a terminal—and everything else supports it.

## Scrolling keeps every window substantial

Scrolling mode follows a different rule: each window opens at roughly 43% of the screen. Instead of shrinking the existing layout to make room, the workspace extends horizontally and moves to reveal the newest window.

That changes the feeling of multitasking. Windows remain wide enough to be useful, and moving through them feels more like navigating a strip of work than managing a grid of rectangles.

## A layout is really a point of view

Dwindle gives you the whole workspace at a glance. Scrolling gives each task more room and brings the current edge of the workspace forward. Neither is the permanent “right” layout; the useful part is being able to change the point of view while the windows stay put.

That is what I like about Omarchy’s approach. The desktop has opinions, but those opinions help the workspace adapt to the work instead of becoming work of its own.
